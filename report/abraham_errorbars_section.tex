\section{Error Bars When Rows Share People and Events}
\label{sec:errorbars}

Every number in Section~5 is a cross-validated estimate, and every one needs an error bar. The textbook's error bars
for cross-validation treat the rows as independent \cite[Section 5.1.3, pp.~206--208]{James:2023}. Ours are not: a
participant appears in up to 22 dyads, and all dyads in an event share the same evening. This section asks which
error bars are honest for such data, proves what the common ones estimate, and tests every prediction on simulated
data built like ours. Each prediction was committed to our repository before its simulation ran.

\subsection{Setup and notation}

There are $G=21$ events. Event $g$ contains $n_g$ dyads and $n=\sum_g n_g=4{,}184$. A fitted model $\hat f$ assigns each
dyad $i$ a loss $\ell_i$; we use the misclassification loss with the threshold at the training match rate. In
\emph{grouped} (event) $K$-fold cross-validation the events are dealt at random into $K$ folds; the model
$\hat f_{-k}$ is fitted on the events outside fold $k$ and scores the dyads in fold $k$. The cross-validation estimate is
the mean held-out loss,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{\mathrm{CV}}=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in F_k}\ell\big(y_i,\hat f_{-k}(x_i)\big).
\end{equation}
The quantity a matchmaker cares about is the error of the model fitted to all the data on a new event, with new
people,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}=E\big[\ell\big(Y,\hat f(X)\big)\,\big|\,\text{data}\big],\qquad (X,Y)\ \text{from a new event}.
\end{equation}
A 90\% interval for the error is \emph{honest} if it contains $\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}$ about 90\% of the time over repetitions of
the whole study.

\subsection{What each cross-validation scheme estimates}

\begin{lem}[Lemma 1 of our theory note]\label{lem:what}
Suppose events are independent and identically distributed and the learner treats its training rows symmetrically.
With grouped folds, for every dyad $i$ in fold $k$,
\[
E\big[\ell_i\,\big|\,\text{training events of fold }k\big]=\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}\big(\hat f_{-k}\big),
\]
so $\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{\mathrm{CV}}$ estimates the new-event error of a model trained on $(K-1)/K$ of the events. With random-row folds
it instead estimates the error on rows from events (and people) the model has already seen.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
With grouped folds the event of dyad $i$ is disjoint from the training events of fold $k$, so $(x_i,y_i)$ is
independent of $\hat f_{-k}$ and has the law of a dyad from a new event. Conditioning on the training events gives the
display; averaging over $i$ and $k$ gives the claim. Under random-row folds the event of dyad $i$ usually has other
dyads in the training set, so the same argument holds only conditionally on that event, which is the
already-seen-event error.
\end{proof}

The difference is not academic. With random-row folds every model's AUC rises, by 0.08 to 0.13 on the \texttt{pre}
features and by 0.02 to 0.07 on \texttt{pre+date} (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}): the model partly recognizes people it
met in training. Grouped cross-validation for clustered, spatial or hierarchical data is recommended for the same
reason in ecology \cite[pp.~917, 919]{Roberts:2017}.

\subsection{Three kinds of error bar}

\emph{Rows independent.} The usual standard error of a mean, $\hat V_{\mathrm{naive}}=s^2/n$ with $s^2$ the sample variance of the
$\ell_i$. It ignores the correlation of losses within an event, which inflates the true variance by roughly the
design effect $1+(\bar m-1)\rho$, where $\bar m$ is the cluster size and $\rho$ the within-cluster correlation of the
losses \cite{Kish:1965}, \cite[Section II.B, Eq.~(6)]{Cameron:2015}.

\emph{Cluster-robust.} Sum the residuals within each event, then square:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vcr}
\hat V_{\mathrm{CR}}=\frac{G}{G-1}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\Big(\sum_{i\in g}(\ell_i-\bar\ell)\Big)^2 .
\end{equation}
This is the sandwich variance of Liang and Zeger \cite{Liang:1986} applied to a mean, with the usual small-sample
factor $G/(G-1)$ \cite[Section II.C, Eq.~(12)]{Cameron:2015}.
It allows any correlation within an event and none between events. With only 21 events it should be used with $t$
quantiles on $G-1=20$ degrees of freedom, or replaced by the bias-reduced CR2 version \cite[Section 3]{Bell:2002} or
the wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ \cite{Cameron:2008}, which Cameron and Miller recommend when clusters are few
\cite[Sections VI.B--VI.D]{Cameron:2015}.
For the AUC we use the clustered version of DeLong's placement-value variance due to Obuchowski
\cite{DeLong:1988,Obuchowski:1997}.

\emph{Repeated cross-validation.} Repeat $K$-fold cross-validation $R$ times, collect the $J=RK$ fold errors $e_1,\dots,e_J$,
and use Nadeau and Bengio's corrected variance \cite[Section 3.1, p.~248]{Nadeau:2003}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nb}
\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{NB}}=\Big(\frac1J+\frac{n_{\mathrm{test}}}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\Big)S_e^2=\Big(\frac1J+\frac{1}{K-1}\Big)S_e^2,
\end{equation}
where $S_e^2$ is the sample variance of the fold errors. The second term is a rough allowance for the overlap of the
training sets, since no unbiased estimator of the variance of $K$-fold cross-validation exists
\cite[Theorem 6]{Bengio:2004}.

\begin{prop}[Proposition 6 of our theory note]\label{prop:nb}
Hold the losses $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n$ fixed, in $G=Kq$ events of equal size, and let $\bar c_g$ be the event means and
$s_c^2=\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_g(\bar c_g-\bar\ell)^2$. Average $\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{NB}}$ over random partitions. If the folds are grouped by event,
\[
E\big[\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{NB}}\big]=\frac{J+K-1}{J-1}\cdot\frac{s_c^2}{G}=\frac{J+K-1}{J-1}\cdot\hat V_{\mathrm{CR}};
\]
if the folds are random rows, the same holds with $s_c^2/G$ replaced by the naive variance $s^2/n$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Each partition's fold means average to $\bar\ell$ exactly, so $E[S_e^2]=\frac{J}{J-1}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathrm{sel}}(e_j)$, where $\operatorname{Var}_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is the
variance over the random choice of fold. A grouped fold mean is the mean of $q$ event means drawn without
replacement from $G$, so its selection variance is $(1-q/G)\,s_c^2/q=(K-1)s_c^2/G$. Multiplying by
$\frac1J+\frac{1}{K-1}=\frac{J+K-1}{J(K-1)}$ gives the result. For row folds replace event means by rows. With equal event sizes,
$s_c^2/G$ equals \eqref{eq:vcr}.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:nb} says two things. On grouped folds, the Nadeau--Bengio correction carries the cluster-robust
variance, so it handles the clustering; on random-row folds it inherits the naive variance and fails by the design
effect. For $J=K$ it reduces to the random-group variance estimator of survey sampling \cite[Chapter 2]{Wolter:2007}.

\begin{prop}[Proposition 8 of our theory note; first order]\label{prop:first}
Let the model be fitted by an $M$-estimator with per-event influence functions $\psi_g$, let $a_g$ be event $g$'s mean
loss at the limiting parameter, and let $\beta_g=d^\top\psi_g$, where $d$ is the gradient of the expected loss. For balanced
events and $G\to\infty$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{\mathrm{CV}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}=\bar a-E[a]+o_p(G^{-1/2})$, so $\hat V_{\mathrm{CR}}$ is first-order right for the error of the
cross-validation estimate as an estimate of $\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}$;
\item[(ii)] $G\,E[\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{NB}}]\to\frac{J+K-1}{J-1}\operatorname{Var}\big(a-\beta/(K-1)\big)$, so the Nadeau--Bengio interval is conservative
whenever $\operatorname{Var}(\beta)>2(K-1)\operatorname{Cov}(a,\beta)$;
\item[(iii)] when the evaluation loss is the training loss (log-loss), $d=0$ and the ratio in (ii) is exactly 1.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

The derivation is a first-order Taylor expansion of each fold's error around the limiting parameter; it is in
\texttt{proposals/06}, Section 6e, with its assumptions. Part (i) agrees with the central limit theorem of Bayle
et al.\ \cite[Theorem 1]{Bayle:2020} applied with events as the units, and with Wager's observation that the conditional risk
moves at lower order \cite{Wager:2020}; part (ii), the limit of the Nadeau--Bengio variance, is to our knowledge new.

\subsection{Simulation design}

Following Morris, White and Crowther \cite[Sections 3--5]{Morris:2019}, we simulated data sets with the structure of ours and recorded how often each 90\% interval contained
$\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}$. In the realistic design the truth is a logistic model for the match fitted to our data, with a
woman effect, a man effect and an event effect (person SD in $\{0,0.5,1\}$, event SD in $\{0,0.4\}$); each replicate draws
fresh events (10, 21 or 40), fresh people and fresh outcomes, and $\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is the full model's error on 2,000 new
events. A balanced synthetic design with $G$ from 20 to 320 tests the asymptotic claims. Every design used 300 to 400
replicates, so a coverage of 90\% carries a Monte Carlo standard error of about 1.5 points
\cite[Section 5.2, Table 6]{Morris:2019}. Two lessons from the theory shaped the design: a coverage study must redraw the events in every
replicate, or it overstates the cluster-robust interval's coverage (Proposition 7 of the theory note), and the target
must be scored on many new events, or its own Monte Carlo noise depresses the measured coverage.

\subsection{The cross-validated AUC}

The AUC is not a mean of per-dyad losses, and the usual cross-validated AUC pools the held-out scores of all $K$ fold
models into one ranking. Pooling is known to bias the AUC downward with independent rows
\cite{Parker:2007}, \cite[Section 2.4]{Forman:2010}, \cite[Section 3]{Airola:2011}.
We showed that clustering magnifies this bias from order $1/n$ to order $1/G$. In a pair of dyads compared across
folds, each side's fold model was fitted on the other side's event, which shifts the comparison against the true
ordering. A second-order expansion (Proposition 9 of the theory note) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mix}
E\big[\text{pooled AUC}-\text{BCWF-pooled AUC}\big]=\frac{1}{G-1}\Big(C_{\mathrm{mix}}+\frac{c_2}{K-1}\Big)+o(1/G),
\end{equation}
where BCWF-pooled is the AUC over match and non-match pairs from \emph{different events within the same fold}, and
$C_{\mathrm{mix}}<0$ and $c_2$ are population constants we compute numerically. The leading term does not shrink with more
folds.

\begin{prop}[Proposition 10 of our theory note]\label{prop:half}
If the dyads are independent and the logistic model is correctly specified, then $c_2=C_{\mathrm{mix}}/2$ exactly, so the pooling
bias of $K$-fold cross-validation is $\frac{2K-1}{2(K-1)}\,C_{\mathrm{mix}}/(G-1)$ to leading order.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Write $s$ for the score, $f_1,f_0$ for its densities among matches and non-matches, and $Q(a,b)=\tilde\mu(a)^\top H^{-1}\tilde\mu(b)$
with $\tilde\mu(s)=E[\tilde x\mid s]$ and $H$ the Fisher information. Then $c_2=-I'(0)/m$ with
$I(t)=\int f_1(s+t)f_0(s)Q(s+t,s)\,ds$. Averaging the two ways of differentiating $I$ at $t=0$ and using the symmetry of
$Q$ gives $I'(0)=\frac12\int(f_1'f_0-f_1f_0')Q=\frac12\int f_1f_0\,(\operatorname{logit}P(y=1\mid s))'\,Q$, and the derivative of the logit
is 1 under a correct logistic model. The same integral without the factor $\frac12$ is $-mC_{\mathrm{mix}}$. The full proof is
in \texttt{proposals/06}, Section 6f.
\end{proof}

With clustering the identity $c_2=C_{\mathrm{mix}}/2$ is false in general (a two-point random intercept gives a ratio of 0.454),
but it holds to within 0.5\% for Gaussian event effects; the counterexamples are reproduced by
\texttt{scripts/auc\_ratio\_quadrature.py}.

\caption{Pooling held-out scores across fold models biases the AUC downward under clustering; pairs within folds fix
it. (a) Bias against the new-cluster AUC, with the prediction of \eqref{eq:mix} dashed; (b) coverage of 90\% intervals

In simulation (Figure~\ref{fig:aucbias}) the pooled AUC was biased downward in all 12 cells with event effects, by up
to 0.040, about one standard error at 20 events, and \eqref{eq:mix} matched the observed mixing bias to within 1.3\% to
5.1\%. At 10 folds the prediction held to within 1.4\%; at 2 folds with strong event effects it over-predicted by
18.6\%, so that registered test failed (a larger rerun put the shortfall at 5.8\%, the rest coming from a third-order
term). BCWF-pooled was unbiased to within 0.11 standard errors, and its interval covered 89.9\% on average with 40 or
more events. On our data the two estimates agree on average over 20 event partitions (difference $-0.004$ to $+0.002$),
and plugging our data's constants into \eqref{eq:mix} predicts a bias of $-0.0010$ for the pre-date logistic model,
so the AUCs in Section~5 stand.

\subsection{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Hold out whole events (Lemma~\ref{lem:what}); random-row folds answer a different question.
\item Report the cluster-robust standard error with $t_{G-1}$ quantiles, or the wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ when event sizes
are very unequal.
\item The Nadeau--Bengio correction is safe on event folds and conservative by the factor in
Proposition~\ref{prop:first}(ii) for the error rate; never use it on random-row folds.
\item For the AUC, do not pool held-out scores across fold models when events differ in match rate; use BCWF-pooled
with the clustered standard error.
\item In coverage studies, redraw the events and score the target on many new events.
\end{enumerate}

Most ingredients are known: grouped cross-validation, cluster-robust variances, the Nadeau--Bengio correction and
the pooling bias of the AUC. What we add is the analysis of these tools together under clustering: Proposition~\ref{prop:nb}
(the random-group reading of the corrected $t$ on grouped folds), the limit in Proposition~\ref{prop:first}(ii) with its
out-of-sample test, and the order-$1/G$ theory of the pooled AUC's bias (Proposition~9 and Proposition~\ref{prop:half}).
The propositions have been checked by an adversarial review (\texttt{proposals/09}); our mathematical lead's review
is pending.
