# Practice and guidance for evaluating models on grouped or clustered data (CV error bars and pooled CV AUC)

Scope: how applied fields (medical ML, wearables and activity recognition, EEG/BCI, neuroimaging, ecology, education) evaluate models on grouped data, what published guidance says, which software defaults matter, and whether anyone has measured on real data how much error bars or AUCs change once clustering is accounted for. The goal is to judge who would benefit from the results in `proposals/08-cv-error-bars-note.tex`: random-row CV overstates performance; naive error bars are too narrow while cluster-robust t and wild cluster bootstrap-t intervals are honest; Nadeau-Bengio and Bates-Hastie-Tibshirani nested CV with random-row folds under-cover; pooling held-out scores across grouped folds biases the AUC low when clusters differ in outcome rate, and a within-fold between-cluster estimator fixes it.

How sources were checked: bibliographic details (title, authors, year, venue, DOI) were confirmed through the Crossref API for every DOI below. Content claims come from the full text or abstract opened through PubMed Central/Europe PMC, arXiv, publisher-hosted PDFs or official documentation. Where only the bibliographic record was confirmed, the entry says "content not opened". Sources I could not open are marked UNVERIFIED. No DOI here was guessed. Each one resolved in Crossref.

## Q1. How common is subject-wise or grouped CV, and how do papers aggregate the AUC and report error bars, field by field?

### Takeaway
In every field covered, meta-research shows that leaving whole subjects out is recommended but often not done: 45% of digital-health papers, most deep-learning EEG papers, and over half of CNN Alzheimer's MRI papers had subject-level leakage. Where grouped CV is used, the typical report is a single mean, or a mean ± SD across folds, or a pooled out-of-fold AUC whose computation is usually not stated. Cluster-aware confidence intervals for a CV metric are rare: one of 221 MICCAI 2023 segmentation papers reported any CI, and none of 87 surveyed learning-analytics experiments reported fold-level variability. No meta-research I found counts how often papers pool held-out scores across grouped folds versus averaging per-fold AUCs. Pelánek (education) and Forman and Scholz (general ML) do show that the aggregation choice is usually left unstated.

### Cited Findings

**Medical ML and digital health (repeated records per patient)**
- In a systematic review of 62 papers that used smartphone or wearable data for clinical prediction, 28 (about 45%) used record-wise CV and 34 used subject-wise CV. Among the 47 papers with extractable accuracy, the median classification error was 5.6% for record-wise papers and 13.0% for subject-wise papers (P < 0.01). — [Saeb, Lonini, Jayaraman, Mohr, Kording 2017, GigaScience 6(5):gix019, doi:10.1093/gigascience/gix019](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019)
- In Saeb et al.'s real-data demonstration (UCI activity data, random forest), record-wise CV error stayed near 2%, while subject-wise error fell from 27% with 2 subjects to about 7–9% with 30 subjects. Record-wise CV therefore understated error roughly 3–4 fold. — [Saeb et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019)
- On smartphone voice recordings for Parkinson's disease, record-wise splitting and record-wise CV overestimated classifier performance and underestimated error relative to a subject-independent holdout. The authors conclude that record-wise techniques should be avoided in diagnostic settings (abstract opened via Europe PMC, PMC8369053). — [Tougui, Jilbab, El Mhamdi 2021, Healthcare Informatics Research 27(3):189–199, doi:10.4258/hir.2021.27.3.189](https://doi.org/10.4258/hir.2021.27.3.189)
- Reporting completeness in ML clinical prediction models: of 152 supervised-ML prediction-model studies (2018–2019), 91.4% reported some internal validation and 69.7% reported discrimination. Only 5.9% (95% CI 3.1–10.9) completely reported the model's predictive performance, which is TRIPOD item 16; in TRIPOD 2015 that item asks for performance measures with CIs. — [Andaur Navarro et al. 2022, BMC Medical Research Methodology, doi:10.1186/s12874-021-01469-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-021-01469-6) (full text via PMC8759172)

**Medical imaging (several scans, slices or images per patient)**
- In a systematic review of more than 30 papers on CNN classification of Alzheimer's disease from anatomical MRI, more than half may have suffered from data leakage, including slice- or image-level splits that put one subject in both train and test. — [Wen et al. 2020, Medical Image Analysis 63:101694, doi:10.1016/j.media.2020.101694](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2020.101694) (abstract opened via arXiv 1904.07773)
- On three open OCT datasets, improper (non-patient-level) splitting inflated performance by 0.07 to 0.43 in MCC, or 5–30 percentage points in accuracy. The authors add that much of the literature overlooks the split level. — [Tampu, Eklund, Haj-Hosseini 2022, Scientific Data 9:580, doi:10.1038/s41597-022-01618-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01618-6)
- Of 221 MICCAI 2023 segmentation papers, more than 50% did not assess performance variability at all, and only one (0.5%) reported confidence intervals. The median reconstructed 95% CI width (0.03 Dice) was about three times the median gap between first- and second-ranked methods. In more than 60% of papers, the runner-up's mean fell inside the winner's CI. — [Christodoulou, Reinke, …, Varoquaux, Colliot, Maier-Hein 2024, MICCAI 2024; arXiv:2409.17763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17763)
- An example of current good practice, and a claim about field practice: a 2026 preprint on contrast-enhanced mammography (1,880 images, 308 patients) says most published classifiers in that area split CV folds at the image level. The preprint itself uses repeated patient-grouped nested CV with a patient-cluster bootstrap for its AUC CI. This is a single unreviewed preprint. — [Antonijevic, Vidakovic 2026, arXiv:2607.02394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02394)

**Wearables and human activity recognition**
- k-fold CV inflated activity-recognition accuracy by about 10% relative to subject CV, and by about 16% with overlapping sliding windows (arXiv preprint, no journal reference listed). — [Dehghani, Glatard, Shihab 2019, "Subject Cross Validation in Human Activity Recognition", arXiv:1904.02666](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.02666)
- Saeb et al.'s 62-paper review (above) is the main counted evidence for wearables and smartphones. 45% of those papers used record-wise CV. — [Saeb et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019)
- Related, content not opened: Hammerla and Plötz, "Let's (not) stick together: pairwise similarity biases cross-validation in activity recognition", UbiComp 2015, pp. 1041–1051, [doi:10.1145/2750858.2807551](https://doi.org/10.1145/2750858.2807551). Only the bibliographic record was confirmed.

**EEG and BCI**
- Of 63 translational DNN-EEG papers that tried to identify a clinical disorder, only 17 (27.0%) clearly avoided subject leakage; most used segment-based holdout. In the authors' Alzheimer's experiment, segment-based holdout gave 99.8% (99.1–100.0%) accuracy. Subject-based holdout gave 53.0% (43.1–64.8%), a CI that includes chance. Several authors are employed by SPARK Neuro (declared). — [Brookshire et al. 2024, Frontiers in Neuroscience, doi:10.3389/fnins.2024.1373515](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2024.1373515) (full text via PMC11099244)
- In a systematic review of 154 DL-EEG papers (2010–2018): 61% studied only inter-subject classification, 25% only intra-subject, and 8.3% both. About 20% of the 108 inter-subject studies used leave-one- or leave-N-subjects-out validation. 60% of studies used no form of CV, and 19.9% used any statistical test against baselines. — [Roy, Banville, Albuquerque, Gramfort, Falk, Faubert 2019, Journal of Neural Engineering 16(5):051001, doi:10.1088/1741-2552/ab260c](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ab260c) (full text via ar5iv of arXiv:1901.05498; the review's figure 12(b) breakdown of CV types was not readable in text form)

**Neuroimaging (connectome prediction)**
- Across four datasets and three phenotypes: leakage through repeated measurements of the same subject ("subject leakage") greatly inflated prediction performance. "Family leakage" (relatives split across train and test) had little effect in some settings. Most participants in those datasets had no relatives in the data. — [Rosenblatt, Tejavibulya, Jiang, Noble, Scheinost 2024, Nature Communications 15:1829, doi:10.1038/s41467-024-46150-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46150-w) (full text via PMC10901797)
- Across 642 neuroimaging prediction studies compiled from several reviews, the median sample size was 89. — [Varoquaux 2018, NeuroImage 180:68–77, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.06.061](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.06.061) (full text via arXiv:1706.07581)

**Ecology and spatial mapping**
- In forest-biomass mapping (random forest; inventory of 11.8 million trees in central Africa), standard non-spatial validation suggested the model explains more than half of biomass variation. Spatial validation that accounts for spatial autocorrelation showed almost no predictive power. — [Ploton et al. 2020, Nature Communications 11:4540, doi:10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y) (abstract opened)
- A Comment on global ML maps notes that none of the global mapping studies it discusses used probability-sampled reference data, and that the data are heavily clustered. Random k-fold or leave-one-out CV is appropriate only for i.i.d. data; otherwise it gives biased, overoptimistic assessment, and spatial CV strategies are needed to control the dependence. It also records the counter-position of Wadoux et al. 2021, that spatial CV is not the right way to evaluate map accuracy. — [Meyer & Pebesma 2022, Nature Communications 13:2208, doi:10.1038/s41467-022-29838-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29838-9) (full text via PMC9033849); counter-position: [Wadoux, Heuvelink, de Bruin, Brus 2021, Ecological Modelling 457:109692, doi:10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2021.109692](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2021.109692) (content not opened; characterized via Meyer & Pebesma)

**Education and learning analytics**
- A survey of 87 MOOC predictive-modeling experiments from flagship venues found CV the dominant evaluation, mostly 2-, 5- or 10-fold. In no case was fold-level performance reported or used to estimate variance; performance was reported only as an average. The authors argue that common NHST practice, such as ANOVA over CV results, is unsuitable because the independence assumption fails, and they propose a Friedman/Nemenyi procedure or Bayesian hierarchical comparison across courses. — [Gardner & Brooks 2018, Journal of Learning Analytics 5(2):105–125, doi:10.18608/jla.2018.52.7](https://doi.org/10.18608/jla.2018.52.7) (full text via arXiv:1801.08494)
- In student modeling, AUC is computed in at least three ways: globally pooled over all answers, averaged per skill, or averaged per student. These weight students differently, and papers usually do not say which they used. The large improvement claimed for deep knowledge tracing over earlier work on the same data was probably due in large part to global versus per-skill AUC computation, as Khajah et al. had noticed. Some papers compute AUC per student and report averages and statistical comparisons. — [Pelánek 2017, "Measuring Predictive Performance of User Models: The Details Matter", UMAP'17 Adjunct, pp. 197–201, doi:10.1145/3099023.3099042](https://doi.org/10.1145/3099023.3099042) (full PDF opened); extended version: [Pelánek 2018, UMUAI, doi:10.1007/s11257-018-9204-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-018-9204-y) (content not opened)
- A broader review of student-success prediction in MOOCs: [Gardner & Brooks 2018, UMUAI 28(2):127–203, doi:10.1007/s11257-018-9203-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-018-9203-z). It was opened via arXiv:1711.06349 but gave no counts relevant here.

**General ML: pooled versus fold-averaged AUC**
- Forman and Scholz define AUC_merge, which sorts all folds' scores into one ROC, and AUC_avg, the mean of fold AUCs. They note that AUC_merge implicitly requires calibration across folds and downgrades good rankers whose calibration varies between folds. An informal survey of dozens of articles found disagreement and, mostly, no statement of which method was used. WEKA 3.6.1 used AUC_merge in its Explorer GUI and core Evaluation class but AUC_avg in its Experimenter. — [Forman & Scholz 2010, ACM SIGKDD Explorations 12(1):49–57, doi:10.1145/1882471.1882479](https://doi.org/10.1145/1882471.1882479) (full PDF opened)
- Related, content not opened: Airola, Pahikkala, Waegeman, De Baets, Salakoski 2011, "An experimental comparison of cross-validation techniques for estimating the area under the ROC curve", Computational Statistics & Data Analysis 55(4):1828–1844, [doi:10.1016/j.csda.2010.11.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csda.2010.11.018). A search-engine summary attributed to it a negative bias of pooled K-fold AUC and high variance of fold-averaged AUC on small data. I did not open the paper, so treat that content as UNVERIFIED.

**Cross-field leakage meta-research**
- A survey of 22 papers across 17 fields found leakage affecting 294 papers, which the authors treat as a lower bound. Their taxonomy includes "non-independence between train and test" (L3.2), where samples come from the same people or units, and they recommend block CV for dependent data. In their civil-war case study, 9 of 12 reproducible papers reported no significance tests or uncertainty quantification. One headline AUC of 0.85 had a bootstrap 95% CI of 0.66–0.95 on a test set with 11 positive cases. — [Kapoor & Narayanan 2023, Patterns 4(9):100804, doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804) (full text via PMC10499856)

### Inferences
- The point-estimate problem (random-row CV overstates performance) is well documented in every field covered. Its magnitude has been measured on real data many times: about 2.3x lower median error in record-wise papers (Saeb); about 10–16% accuracy inflation in human activity recognition; 99.8% versus 53% in EEG; 5–30 points of accuracy in OCT; "more than half" versus "quasi-null" explained variance in ecology. The note's Lemma 1 adds no new practical message here, though it gives a clean statement of which estimand each scheme targets.
- The uncertainty step is where practice is thin. Most fields report a bare mean or mean ± SD of fold scores (education: no fold-level variance at all; MICCAI: >50% no variability, 0.5% CIs). Some report DeLong or bootstrap CIs computed at the row level. Patient-cluster bootstraps appear but are not standard. This is the gap the cluster-robust and wild-cluster-bootstrap results address.
- The pooled-versus-averaged AUC choice is rarely reported in any field (Forman & Scholz; Pelánek). Readers therefore usually cannot tell whether a published grouped-CV AUC is subject to the downward pooling bias of Proposition 9. That bias would matter most where clusters differ in outcome rate: patients with different event rates, sites with different prevalence, students with different success rates, events of different composition.
- Fields with few, large clusters (hospitals, regions) are steered by TRIPOD-Cluster toward per-cluster performance plus random-effects meta-analysis (Q2). Fields with many small clusters (patients with a few records, subjects with many EEG segments, students) mostly pool rows. The note's estimators sit between these regimes. The latter group is where pooled AUCs and row-level error bars are most common.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-research that counts, in any field, how often a grouped-CV AUC is pooled (`cross_val_predict` style) versus averaged over folds. The evidence is qualitative: Forman & Scholz's informal survey and Pelánek's analysis of student-modeling papers.
- I found no meta-research for wearables, ecology or BCI that tabulates which error bar is reported (fold SD, SEM, DeLong, bootstrap, none). Roy et al.'s figure on CV types and statistics was not readable in text form.
- No education study I found quantifies student-level or class-level leakage the way Saeb or Brookshire do. Pelánek covers metric aggregation, not fold grouping.

## Q2. What do the named guidance sources recommend for CV with grouped data and for uncertainty in CV metrics?

### Takeaway
The guidance agrees on the split: hold out the unit you will generalize to (subject, site, block). It is either silent or generic on the error bar for a grouped-CV metric. TRIPOD+AI asks for performance with CIs and points clustered studies to TRIPOD-Cluster. TRIPOD-Cluster recommends internal-external CV with a random-effects meta-analysis of per-cluster performance, which suits a few large clusters. Varoquaux 2018 shows that fold-based standard errors understate CV uncertainty. No source tells practitioners how to get an interval for a grouped-CV metric with many small clusters, or warns that pooling scores across grouped folds biases the AUC.

### Cited Findings
- **Saeb et al. 2017.** For diagnosis with a global model, use subject-wise CV, because record-wise CV lets the classifier exploit subject identity. Tune hyperparameters on subsets of the training data. Splitting by time can be right for prognosis with personal models. For multi-site generalization, CV should span sites. The paper reports CIs and SDs in its figures but makes no explicit recommendation about error bars. — [doi:10.1093/gigascience/gix019](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019)
- **Little et al. 2017 (companion perspectives on Saeb et al.).** Max Little argues that subject-wise CV is not always correct: for identity-clustered i.i.d. mixture data it need not estimate out-of-sample error consistently, and the right scheme depends on the dependence structure. Varoquaux's part agrees that a method meant for new patients must be tested on new subjects, while prognosis from continuous measurements may leave out future measurements instead. It also recommends test sets of 10–20% of the data rather than leave-one-out. — [Little, Varoquaux, Saeb, Lonini, Jayaraman, Mohr, Kording 2017, GigaScience 6(5), doi:10.1093/gigascience/gix020](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix020) (full text via PMC5441396)
- **Roberts et al. 2017 (Ecography).** Random CV can seriously underestimate predictive error when data have temporal, spatial, hierarchical or phylogenetic structure. Block CV was nearly always more appropriate when predicting to new data, and should be used where dependence exists even if residuals look uncorrelated. Blocking can also force extrapolation and overestimate interpolation error. — [Roberts, Bahn, Ciuti, Boyce, Elith, Guillera-Arroita, Hauenstein, Lahoz-Monfort, Schröder, Thuiller, Warton, Wintle, Hartig, Dormann 2017, Ecography 40(8):913–929, doi:10.1111/ecog.02881](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881). The full 14-author list was confirmed via Crossref. The full text was not opened (publisher returned 403); the content comes from abstract and catalogue excerpts. Treat details beyond the abstract-level claims as UNVERIFIED.
- **Fold-generation tooling in ecology.** blockCV generates spatially or environmentally separated folds for k-fold CV of species distribution models. It makes folds; it is not an inference tool. — [Valavi, Elith, Lahoz-Monfort, Guillera-Arroita 2019, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 10(2):225–232, doi:10.1111/2041-210X.13107](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.13107) (bibliographic record only)
- **TRIPOD+AI (2024).**
  - Item 12c asks authors to specify the model-building steps and the method of internal validation; the glossary lists train-test split, CV and bootstrap.
  - Item 12d asks whether and how heterogeneity in model parameters and performance across clusters (e.g., hospitals, countries) was handled and quantified.
  - Item 23a asks for performance estimates with confidence intervals, including for key subgroups.
  - Item 23b asks for any examined heterogeneity in performance across clusters.
  - Item 26 asks authors to discuss statistical uncertainty among the limitations.
  - Studies that account for clustering are told to consult TRIPOD-Cluster.
  - The statement text retrieved from PMC never uses the word "leakage", and it does not say how to compute a CI for a cross-validated metric or how to aggregate fold AUCs.
  - Source: [Collins, Moons, Dhiman, Riley, Beam, Van Calster, Ghassemi, Liu, Reitsma, van Smeden, Boulesteix, Camaradou, et al. 2024, BMJ 385:e078378, doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-078378](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078378) (full text via PMC11019967)
- **TRIPOD-Cluster (2023), explanation and elaboration.**
  - It recommends internal-external cross-validation (IECV), which leaves out one cluster at a time, as more informative than a random or single non-random split.
  - Performance is summarized across clusters by random-effects meta-analysis (REML, Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman CIs, τ², I², and prediction intervals for new settings), with forest plots of cluster-specific performance.
  - Each omitted cluster should be large enough for validation.
  - Bootstrapping or IECV that omits the model-building steps ignores model uncertainty and gives overoptimistic results.
  - The c statistic depends on case-mix homogeneity and on outcome prevalence within a cluster.
  - Source: [Debray, Collins, Riley, et al. 2023, BMJ 380:e071058, doi:10.1136/bmj-2022-071058](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2022-071058) (full text via PMC9903176); checklist: [BMJ 2023;380:e071018, doi:10.1136/bmj-2022-071018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2022-071018) (cited by TRIPOD+AI; not opened)
- **PROBAST+AI (2025).** Its glossary defines data leakage as development data entering the evaluation phase, and says evaluation data should be chosen so that participants do not overlap with training data. It separates imprecision (wide CIs from small evaluation samples) from bias. The full text retrieved via PMC never uses the word "cluster". — [Moons, Damen, Kaul, Hooft, Andaur Navarro, Dhiman, Beam, Van Calster, et al. 2025, BMJ, doi:10.1136/bmj-2024-082505](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082505) (full text via PMC11931409)
- **Varoquaux 2018 (NeuroImage).**
  - CV error bars are large at typical neuroimaging sample sizes, about ±10% for 100 samples.
  - The standard error across folds strongly underestimates them because folds are not independent (citing Bengio & Grandvalet 2004). In the paper's simulation, SEM-based bounds were too small by a factor of 0.73 for leave-one-out and 0.26 for 50 repeated 20% splits.
  - Its real-data experiment used leave-one-subject-out or leave-one-run-out CV where data had multiple subjects or runs. It also notes that neuroimaging data have correlation across samples from the same subject.
  - It recommends larger samples and repeated random splits of 10–20% test data.
  - It does not give a cluster-aware variance formula.
  - Source: [doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.06.061](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.06.061)
- **Varoquaux & Cheplygina 2022 (npj Digital Medicine).** Lists repeated measures of one individual split across train and test as a leakage example. It recommends test sets of at least several hundred, ideally thousands, of samples, and confidence intervals on reported metrics. — [doi:10.1038/s41746-022-00592-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-022-00592-y) (full text via PMC9005663)
- **Kapoor & Narayanan 2023 (Patterns).** Proposes "model info sheets", a 21-question template that asks authors to justify clean train-test separation, feature legitimacy, and a test set matching the distribution of scientific interest, including non-independence (L3.2). It recommends block CV for dependent data. It reports missing uncertainty quantification in the civil-war case study but gives no rule for CV error bars. — [doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804)
- **Gardner & Brooks 2018 (education).** Recommends replacing naive averages and ANOVA-style tests with a Friedman/Nemenyi procedure or Bayesian hierarchical model comparison that uses fold-level results across courses. — [doi:10.18608/jla.2018.52.7](https://doi.org/10.18608/jla.2018.52.7)
- **Pelánek 2017 (education).** Recommends stating exactly how a metric was computed: global, per skill or per student. — [doi:10.1145/3099023.3099042](https://doi.org/10.1145/3099023.3099042)
- **Genders et al. 2012 (Radiology tutorial).** Diagnostic accuracy computed on multiple observations per patient is clustered. The tutorial illustrates four approaches: ignoring correlation, variance adjustment, random-effects logistic models, and GEE. It advises accounting for within-patient correlation whenever multiple observations per patient matter to the clinical question. It does not address CV. — [Genders, Spronk, Stijnen, Steyerberg, Lesaffre, Hunink 2012, Radiology 265(3):910–916, doi:10.1148/radiol.12120509](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.12120509) (abstract opened)

### Inferences
- The guidance gives a clear rule for the split and only a generic rule for uncertainty ("report CIs"). A practitioner following TRIPOD+AI with event-, patient- or site-grouped CV must report a CI for a cross-validated metric but gets no method. The note's cluster-robust t and wild cluster bootstrap-t results would fill exactly that hole, together with the warning that Nadeau-Bengio and published nested CV with random-row folds under-cover.
- TRIPOD-Cluster's IECV plus random-effects meta-analysis is the main competing recommendation. It targets heterogeneity across a modest number of large clusters, using per-cluster c statistics, which are within-cluster AUCs. It does not cover (a) many small clusters, where per-cluster AUCs are undefined or noisy, or (b) a single pooled AUC over grouped folds. The note's within-fold between-cluster AUC and its pooling-bias result complement it. A preprint should say clearly that the target differs: a pooled or between-cluster discrimination for a new cluster, versus TRIPOD-Cluster's average within-cluster discrimination.
- The guidance never names the pooling bias. TRIPOD-Cluster's remark that c depends on outcome prevalence within clusters is the closest it comes, and it supports the note's mechanism: clusters that differ in outcome rate.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Roberts et al. 2017 full text (403). Its specific case-study numbers and any discussion of error bars for blocked CV are UNVERIFIED.
- I did not open the CLAIM 2024 update (Tejani et al., Radiology: Artificial Intelligence 2024, doi:10.1148/ryai.240300, found via Europe PMC search), because Europe PMC returned no body text. Whether it requires patient-level partitioning or CIs is UNVERIFIED.
- I did not open the TRIPOD-Cluster checklist paper (e071018) separately; the content above comes from the E&E paper.

## Q3. Which software defaults matter, and do they warn about pooling or clustered error bars?

### Takeaway
The main libraries make both aggregations easy and give no clustered error bars. scikit-learn averages per-fold scores in `cross_val_score`/`cross_validate` and pools in `cross_val_predict`, with a generic warning. mlr3 macro-averages by default, offers micro (pooled) averaging, and pools for ROC curves. pROC gives row-level DeLong or bootstrap CIs with no cluster argument. cvAUC is the exception: it has a cluster-aware influence-curve CI for repeated measures. The new mlr3inferr package implements exactly the families the note finds under-cover under clustering (corrected t, Bayle-style Wald, nested CV), and its README says nothing about groups.

### Cited Findings
- **scikit-learn 1.9.1, `cross_val_predict`.** The docstring warns: "Passing these predictions into an evaluation metric may not be a valid way to measure generalization performance." It explains that results differ from `cross_validate`/`cross_val_score` unless all test sets are the same size and the metric decomposes over samples. `groups` is used only with a Group CV splitter such as `GroupKFold`. — [scikit-learn docs: cross_val_predict](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.cross_val_predict.html)
- **scikit-learn user guide, section 3.1.**
  - `cross_val_score` averages over folds, while `cross_val_predict` returns predictions from several distinct models undistinguished, so it is not an appropriate measure of generalization error. Its suggested uses are visualization and blending.
  - The guide's example summarizes CV by fold mean and standard deviation, and does not say whether that SD is a standard error.
  - The grouped-data section motivates `GroupKFold`/`LeaveOneGroupOut` with medical data with several samples per patient, so that held-out groups are absent from training.
  - Shuffled `GroupKFold` does not account for group sizes.
  - The page has no confidence intervals, no standard errors, no clustered error bars, and no warning that pooling across grouped folds biases AUC.
  - Source: [scikit-learn docs: Cross-validation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)
- **mlr3 1.8.0, `Measure$average`.** The default `"macro"` scores each resampling iteration and aggregates, by mean by default. `"micro"` pools all iterations' predictions into one Prediction and scores once. `"macro_weighted"` weights by sample counts or weights, and `"custom"` lets a measure aggregate itself. — [mlr3 reference: Measure](https://mlr3.mlr-org.com/reference/Measure.html)
- **mlr3 book (chapter 3).** Grouping via the `"group"` column role keeps observations of a group together, motivated by repeated measurements on the same individuals. For AUC, the gap between micro and macro averaging is larger than for classification error because AUC does not decompose over observations. ROC and PR curves on a ResampleResult merge predictions across iterations (micro). Resampling estimates are not independent, which makes inference "less than trivial". The book demonstrates the Bayle et al. (2020) CV interval through mlr3inferr. — [mlr3book: Evaluation and benchmarking](https://mlr3book.mlr-org.com/chapters/chapter3/evaluation_and_benchmarking.html)
- **mlr3inferr 0.2.2 (CRAN, 2026-08-21; Fischer & Schulz-Kümpel).** Implements `ci.con_z` (conservative-z, paired subsampling), `ci.cor_t` (corrected t, subsampling), `ci.holdout`, `ci.ncv` (nested CV) and `ci.wald_cv` (Wald CV, for CV and LOO). It cites the benchmark of Schulz-Kümpel et al., arXiv:2409.18836. Neither the CRAN page nor the README mentions grouped or clustered data. — [CRAN mlr3inferr](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/mlr3inferr/index.html); [GitHub mlr-org/mlr3inferr](https://github.com/mlr-org/mlr3inferr)
- **cvAUC 1.1.4 (Date 2022-01-17; LeDell, Petersen, van der Laan).**
  - `cvAUC()` returns per-fold AUCs (`fold.AUC`) and their average as the cross-validated AUC.
  - `ci.cvAUC()` gives an influence-curve SE and CI for i.i.d. data.
  - `ci.pooled.cvAUC()` takes an `ids` argument for "pooled repeated measures data". There, the clusters (e.g., patients), not the observations, are the independent units.
  - The package does not discuss the bias of pooling scores across folds.
  - Source: [CRAN cvAUC reference manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/cvAUC/cvAUC.pdf); method paper: [LeDell, Petersen, van der Laan 2015, Electronic Journal of Statistics 9(1):1583–1607, doi:10.1214/15-EJS1035](https://doi.org/10.1214/15-EJS1035) (bibliographic record only)
- **pROC 1.19.1 (2026-08-27).**
  - `ci.auc()` offers `method = c("delong", "bootstrap")`. DeLong is the default when possible, using the Sun & Xu algorithm. The bootstrap is stratified by cases and controls by default, with resampling of individual observations.
  - The manual has no cluster or subject argument.
  - Its `cov()` method has an `"obuchowski"` option, which per the manual relates to Obuchowski & McClish; I did not confirm whether that handles clustering.
  - The manual says nothing about CV or pooling.
  - Source: [CRAN pROC reference manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pROC/pROC.pdf)
- **WEKA (historical).** As of version 3.6.1, WEKA's Explorer GUI and core Evaluation class computed the cross-validated AUC by merging folds (pooling), while the Experimenter averaged fold AUCs. — [Forman & Scholz 2010](https://doi.org/10.1145/1882471.1882479)

### Inferences
- Two default paths lead to the two common reports. `cross_val_score(..., cv=GroupKFold, scoring="roc_auc")` gives the fold-average AUC and invites "mean ± SD of folds". `roc_auc_score(y, cross_val_predict(..., cv=GroupKFold))` (or mlr3 `average="micro"`, or WEKA Explorer) gives the pooled AUC. Feeding pooled predictions into pROC's DeLong then gives a row-level CI. Under the note's results, the first path's SD-based bar is too narrow, and the second path is biased low when clusters differ in outcome rate and gets a too-narrow row-level CI as well.
- scikit-learn's warning gives the wrong reason for practitioners worried about clustering. It blames unequal test sizes and non-decomposable metrics, not the between-fold calibration shifts that Forman & Scholz and Proposition 9 identify. A concrete, low-cost improvement would be a docs note that pooled AUC across grouped folds is biased when groups differ in base rate, plus an example of a cluster-robust or cluster-bootstrap interval.
- mlr3inferr is the clearest direct user of the note. It packages corrected-t/Nadeau-Bengio, Wald-CV and nested-CV intervals for general use, and the README is silent on groups. Whether `ci.cor_t`/`ci.ncv` respect the `group` column role, and how they perform under clustering, is untested in anything I found.
- cvAUC's `ci.pooled.cvAUC` shows that cluster-as-unit influence-curve intervals for CV AUC already exist in software (2015). The note's novelty claim for clustered AUC intervals should be framed against it. A precise comparison of what ci.pooled.cvAUC computes inside each fold (all pairs versus between-cluster pairs) was not possible from the manual.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect cvAUC's source to see whether `ci.pooled.cvAUC` computes each fold's AUC over all case-control pairs, including within-cluster pairs, or only between-cluster pairs. This matters for comparison with the note's within-fold between-cluster estimator.
- I did not confirm whether mlr3inferr's methods honor grouped resampling, or whether its `ci.ncv` uses grouped inner folds.
- I did not check tidymodels/rsample (`group_vfold_cv`), caret, or yardstick's macro/micro defaults. These are other common R defaults.

## Q4. Has any paper measured on real data how much grouped-CV error bars or AUCs change when clustering is accounted for?

### Takeaway
Many papers measure on real data how much the point estimate changes when folds respect the groups (Q1). I found no paper that takes a fixed grouped-CV estimate on real data and compares naive or fold-SD error bars with cluster-robust or cluster-bootstrap ones. I also found none that measures the pooled-versus-within-fold AUC gap under grouped folds on real data. The nearest evidence: Varoquaux's real-data CV-versus-holdout discrepancies (about ±10%), Brookshire's subject-holdout CI, a 2026 preprint using a patient-cluster bootstrap, and classical clustered-ROC or diagnostic-accuracy methods (Obuchowski 1997; Genders 2012) used outside CV. The note's real-data error-bar run on the 21 speed-dating events appears to be among the few such measurements, though my searches were not exhaustive.

### Cited Findings
- **Point estimates.** Measured on real data: record-wise versus subject-wise (Saeb: about 2% versus about 7–9% error), k-fold versus subject CV in activity recognition (about +10% to +16%), segment versus subject holdout in EEG (99.8% versus 53.0%), image versus patient split in OCT (+5–30 accuracy points), random versus spatial validation in forest mapping (more than half versus quasi-null explained variance). — [Saeb 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019); [Dehghani 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.02666); [Brookshire 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2024.1373515); [Tampu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01618-6); [Ploton 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y)
- **Error-bar size on real data, no clustering adjustment.** Using nested CV on real neuroimaging data (fMRI within and across subjects, VBM, MEG), CV accuracy differed from accuracy on a large held-out set by roughly −10% to +10% (5th–95th percentile, varying by modality and scheme). The SEM-based shortfall factors (0.73 for LOO, 0.26 for repeated 20% splits) come from the paper's simulation, not from clustered real data. — [Varoquaux 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.06.061)
- **Example of a cluster-aware CV interval in applied work.** A 2026 mammography preprint reports a patient-level AUC of 0.874 (95% CI 0.827–0.918) from repeated patient-grouped nested CV with a patient-cluster bootstrap. It does not report the naive interval for comparison. — [arXiv:2607.02394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02394)
- **Classical clustered-ROC and diagnostic-accuracy methods (not CV).** Obuchowski 1997 gives nonparametric analysis of clustered ROC data. — [Obuchowski 1997, Biometrics 53(2):567–578, doi:10.2307/2533958](https://doi.org/10.2307/2533958) (bibliographic record only). Genders et al. 2012 illustrate variance adjustment, random effects and GEE for clustered sensitivity and specificity. — [doi:10.1148/radiol.12120509](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.12120509)
- **Clustered multicenter prediction (simulation, not real data; content not opened).** Wynants, Vergouwe, Van Huffel, Timmerman, Van Calster, "Does ignoring clustering in multicenter data influence the performance of prediction models? A simulation study", Statistical Methods in Medical Research 27(6):1723–1736 (2018; online 2016), [doi:10.1177/0962280216668555](https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280216668555) (bibliographic record only).
- **Theory on CV for correlated data (content not opened; likely covered by the parallel novelty researcher).** Rabinowicz & Rosset, "Cross-Validation for Correlated Data", JASA 117(538):718–731 (2022), [doi:10.1080/01621459.2020.1801451](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2020.1801451). Bates, Hastie & Tibshirani, "Cross-Validation: What Does It Estimate and How Well Does It Do It?", JASA 119(546):1434–1445 (2024), [doi:10.1080/01621459.2023.2197686](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2023.2197686). Bibliographic records only.
- **A preprint that turned up in search but did not check out.** Roth 2026, "Which Leakage Types Matter? A Quantitative Landscape Across 2,047 Benchmark Datasets", [arXiv:2604.04199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04199), covers i.i.d. tabular data. A search summary attributed to it a claim that CIs are about 1.7x too narrow. That claim does not appear in the abstract, and the abstract describes no grouped-CV comparison. Treat the claim as UNVERIFIED and do not cite it for this point.

### Inferences
- **Who would benefit.** The audiences with the most to gain are those who already group their folds but report a fold SD, a row-level DeLong CI, or a pooled AUC:
  - digital-health and wearable studies using leave-subject-out;
  - DL-EEG studies moving to subject-based holdout;
  - medical-imaging studies with patient-level splits;
  - multi-site neuroimaging with leave-site-out;
  - learning-analytics studies with student-level or course-level folds.

  A second audience is tool builders: scikit-learn docs, mlr3inferr, and the users of cvAUC and pROC.
- **What would change.**
  1. Report a cluster-robust t interval (with t on G−1 or effective-G degrees of freedom) or a wild cluster bootstrap-t interval for grouped-CV metrics, instead of fold SD/SEM or a row-level DeLong interval.
  2. Do not reuse Nadeau-Bengio corrected-t or published nested-CV intervals computed with random-row folds when rows share people, sites or events.
  3. For the AUC under grouped folds with base rates that differ across clusters, report the fold-averaged AUC or the within-fold between-cluster AUC rather than the pooled out-of-fold AUC.
  4. State which aggregation was used, as Pelánek and Forman & Scholz already urge.
- **What a preprint can claim, and what it cannot.** A preprint can credibly say that the guidance (TRIPOD+AI, TRIPOD-Cluster, PROBAST+AI, Kapoor & Narayanan) and the major software (scikit-learn, mlr3, pROC) give no method for, or warning about, clustered CV error bars or the pooled-AUC bias. It should acknowledge three things:
  - cvAUC's `ci.pooled.cvAUC` already gives cluster-as-unit CV-AUC intervals;
  - TRIPOD-Cluster's IECV plus meta-analysis is the medical standard for a few large clusters;
  - Forman & Scholz (2010) and, per the unverified summary, Airola et al. (2011) documented that pooled CV AUC can be biased, though not tied to clustering.

### Gaps
- I found no real-data study comparing naive, fold-SD, cluster-robust and cluster-bootstrap intervals for the same grouped-CV metric. With the search budget I had, I cannot rule out such work in econometrics, survey statistics or recent arXiv preprints.
- I found no real-data study measuring the pooled-versus-fold-averaged (or between-cluster) AUC gap under grouped folds as a function of cluster outcome-rate heterogeneity.
- Obuchowski 1997, LeDell et al. 2015, Wynants et al. 2018, Rabinowicz & Rosset 2022 and Bates et al. 2024 were verified bibliographically, but I did not open their content. Whether any of them reports a real-data naive-versus-clustered comparison of CV error bars is unknown here.
