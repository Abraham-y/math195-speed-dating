# Predicting mutual interest before two strangers meet: midterm written report (draft)

Sophia Qin, Jenna Yang, Abraham Yeung. Math 195. Generated by `scripts/report_midterm.py` from `results/`; edit the wording, not the numbers.

## 1. Question

How well can we predict, from what is known before two strangers meet, whether both will want to see each other again? Which characteristics of the pair matter? And how much does a four-minute conversation add? A second question is statistical. The rows of this data share people and events, so the usual cross-validation error bars are too narrow; we measure by how much, and test which constructions are honest. Every hypothesis was committed to `PREREG.md` before its test ran.

## 2. Data

The Columbia speed-dating experiment (Fisman et al. 2006) has 551 participants (274 women, 277 men) at 21 events. Within an event every woman met every man for four minutes, and each said yes or no. Our unit for predicting a match is the woman-man pair: 4,184 dyads, of which 16.5% were mutual yeses. Participants said yes to 42% of their dates (women 36.5%, men 47.5%). Two feature sets are compared throughout. `pre` holds what is known before the date: each person's sign-up answers, plus pair features such as the age gap, interest correlation, same field, same goal and preference alignment. `pre+date` adds the ratings the two gave each other during the date.

## 3. Methods

Every model is evaluated by **event 5-fold cross-validation**: whole events are held out, so the people in a test fold are strangers to the model. Missing answers are filled with training-fold medians plus missing-value flags, and a complete-case version runs alongside. Classification uses the training folds' match rate as the threshold. We report AUC, balanced accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. Error bars are clustered by event, and each formula was checked on simulated data with the real design before being reported. The full procedure, with code, is in `results/methods_doc.pdf`.

### 4.3 The error-bar study

On the real data, the naive SE of the full logistic model's `pre` error rate is 0.0076. Clustering by event gives 0.0174 (2.3 times), two-way clustering by woman and man gives 0.0189 (2.5 times), and our cluster-nested, bias-adjusted interval gives 0.0201 (2.6 times). In a simulation matched to the data (A2, supported), each 90% interval covered the true error on new events as follows:

| Interval | Coverage | Mean half-width |
| --- | --- | --- |
| random-row CV, naive SE (conventional) | 79.0% | 0.0125 |
| random-row CV, nested (Bates et al.) | 73.6% | 0.0139 |
| event CV, naive SE | 74.6% | 0.0125 |
| event CV, cluster-robust SE | 99.2% | 0.0288 |
| event CV, cluster-nested | 93.8% | 0.0401 |
| event CV, floor of cluster-robust and cluster-nested | 100.0% | 0.0431 |
| random-row nested, bias-adjusted (Bates et al.) | 67.8% | 0.0124 |
| event cluster-nested, bias-adjusted | 92.6% | 0.0359 |

Table 2. 500 simulated data sets with the real design (`sim_cv_matched.py`).

The conventional interval and the published nested-CV interval of Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani (2024) both fall well short. Holding out events fixes what is being estimated. In this design the cluster-robust SE over-covers, and our cluster-nested, bias-adjusted version is closest to 90%. A literature check (`proposals/07`) found no paper that combines grouped folds at both levels, a cluster-robust outer variance and the bias adjustment, and tests its coverage. That combination is the candidate contribution, framed as an extension of Bates et al. to clustered data.

Across 18 simulated designs: **N1** (not supported): cluster-nested bias-adjusted coverage 0.735 to 0.920 over 15 designs; 6 of 15 within 0.85 to 0.95. **N2** (supported): random-row nested bias-adjusted coverage 0.460 to 0.735 over 12 designs; 12 of 12 below 0.80. Our interval covers 86.5% to 92.0% with the real 21 events, but 83.0% to 86.0% with 10 redrawn events and 73.5% to 82.0% with 40. In 4% to 15% of data sets its MSE estimate is zero or negative, which gives a zero-width interval; among the rest it covers 84% to 98%. The cluster-robust SE covers 96.5% to 100.0% with the real events but 84.0% to 94.5% with redrawn ones, so the A2 figures are flattered by holding the events fixed. A post hoc repair, the larger of the cluster-robust and cluster-nested SEs, covered 92% to 100% in every design; it will be pre-registered before it is tested.

### 4.4 Part 3: luck, mutual liking and stable matches

- **R1** (supported): leaders drop 0.814 points on average (SE 0.132) over 42 blocks; 81% of leaders drop. **R2** (supported): empirical-Bayes shrinkage cut the RMSE of predicting the other half from 0.958 to 0.848. The evening's most-liked person is partly lucky.
- **K1** (supported): dyadic reciprocity of liking is 0.208 (90% interval 0.182 to 0.240). Liking splits into the rater (29%), the person rated (15%) and the pair plus noise (56%).
- **K2** (not supported): generalized reciprocity is 0.036 (90% interval -0.021 to 0.085). Our simulation check found this registered estimator biased by +0.034. A corrected estimator, which is exploratory, gives -0.003 (90% interval -0.071 to 0.055).
- **Stable matching** (exploratory): 44% of stable pairs were mutual yeses, and 18% of real matches were stable pairs. The proposing side gets better-ranked partners: men 4.51 against women 4.76 when men propose, and women 4.43 against men 4.87 when women propose.

## 5. What it means

What is known before the date carries a small, real signal. It mostly reflects who tends to like others and who tends to be liked, not who suits whom, and no model choice gets past it. The four-minute meeting carries far more information than everything known beforehand. Standard error bars on such results are far too narrow when people and events repeat; holding out whole events and using event-aware error bars is necessary, not optional.

## 6. Limitations and next steps
